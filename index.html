01July 2026

Ceramic wet saw

A compact wet saw that cuts ceramic rods cleanly to an exact, adjustable length.

  • SolidWorks
  • 3D printing
  • Soldering
  • Fabrication
The problem

Ceramic alumina rods had to be cut to length quickly and cleanly for assembling gate assemblies. Cutting them in our own shop was easier than having them manufactured.

What I did

I started with sketches and research into the best ways to cut ceramics, then modeled several designs in CAD and 3D printed each variation to compare.

  • Wet cutting. Dry cuts weren't clean enough, so I researched and bought several pumps to test. Water first came through a dental floss tip, which I replaced with a Loc-Line nozzle.
  • Reused parts. I took the motor and switch from an older handheld saw and re-soldered the connections to work in the new device.
  • Keeping it dry. I built a sealed, waterproof section for the motor and circuitry and tested several guards to cut down splashing.
  • Recycled water. A chamber at the bottom holds the pump, and drainage holes under the rod return the water to it.
  • Cleaner cuts. A clamp on top reduces the vibration that was making cuts rough.
  • Exact lengths. I measured and added an adjustable end stop so every rod comes out the same length.

02July 2026

Otoscope fiducial aligner

A fixture for visually aligning ceramic cathode plates by their fiducial markers.

  • SolidWorks
  • 3D printing
  • Prototyping
The problem

Alumina plates carrying printed cathodes had to be aligned accurately with the larger metal gate assemblies that hold the sources in a computed tomography machine. The plates' outer edges aren't manufactured precisely enough to align by, so the fiducial markers on each plate have to be lined up visually instead.

What I did

I started with hand sketches, then went through many rounds of CAD, 3D printing and testing, checking each piece as I went.

  • A fit for any gate. The outsides of the gate assemblies aren't consistent, so the fixture couldn't clamp onto them. Instead, raised bits on its surface slide into the slots where the cathodes align, so any gate snaps on and stays put. I tested the fit with a plastic sample gate.
  • A change from testing. My first design screwed the gate down from the top (see the CAD model), but driving in the placement screws after alignment could shift the parts. The snap-on design fixed this and leaves room to add the screws afterward.
  • Making the fiducials easy to see. I tuned the otoscope light's brightness and distance so the screen stays black except where light passes through the ceramic around each fiducial, giving a clear outline to center.
  • Standalone by design. I chose otoscopes with built-in displays so the fixture runs without a computer and can stay in the workshop. It also works with laptop software.
  • Built for the shop. After testing several mounts, I put the otoscopes in a detachable piece below the fixture for easy maintenance, and added extended back legs so the heavy displays can't tip it.

03July 2026

Fiber optic wire cutter

A machine that cuts fiber and wire to length and marks each piece, with a calibration interface I coded myself.

  • Arduino R4 WiFi
  • C++
  • HTML
  • SolidWorks
  • Soldering
The problem

Different types of fiber and wire needed to be cut to length for a computed tomography machine, and each material needed its own length. Every piece also had to be easy to identify and carry marks at specific positions along it.

What I did

I wanted the cutter to be fully customizable. I first thought about buttons with presets, then remembered using an Arduino R4 WiFi in my circuits class and realized it could host a web interface with far more control. I started by sketching the physical parts, then built them around that idea.

  • Feeding the fiber. A stepper motor turns a V-grooved drive wheel that pulls the cable off the spool, and the length comes from counting the motor's turns. A wheel on top presses on the cable, wrapped in grip tape I found to reduce slipping.
  • Marking. My first sketch used an arm to control the marker. I changed that to a servo and a spring that move the marker sideways on and off the moving cable.
  • Cutting. The blade sits after the marking station and is motor-controlled.
  • The web interface. I built the interface, which also handles calibration. It sets the length of each piece, the slack before the first mark, and the number of marks. Marks can be evenly spaced with a preset or entered by hand for uneven spacing. It also sets the quantity, such as five of the same cut.
  • Electronics. I wrote the C++ and HTML for the interface and soldered the circuitry myself.
