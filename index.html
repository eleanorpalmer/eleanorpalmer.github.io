Ceramic wet saw

A compact wet saw that cuts ceramic rods cleanly to an exact, adjustable length.

  • SolidWorks
  • 3D printing
  • Soldering
  • Fabrication
  • Lathe & Mill work
The problem

I needed to cut Ceramic Alumina rods to clean, repeatable lengths to be used as separators in a more complex part assembly for a computed tomography machine. Cutting the rods on-site was preferable over having them manufactured, as it allowed for rapid iterations and faster testing of the larger part assembly.

What I did

I started with sketches and research into the best ways to cut ceramics. Then I modeled several designs in CAD, 3D printed, and worked in the shop to adjust the metal components. I tested each iteration of my design on sample rods and made revisions until the saw cut smoothly and accurately.

  • Wet cutting. Dry cutting would not result in clean cuts; the rods chipped. So I researched and bought several water pumps to test, as well as nozzles/tubing types, eventually landing on a Loc-Line Nozzle.
  • Sourcing parts. I took the motor and switch from an older handheld saw and re-soldered the connections to work in the new device. I sourced the other parts from McMaster-Carr.
  • Keeping it dry. I built a sealed, waterproof section for the motor and circuitry and tested several blade guards to cut down splashing.
  • Accurate cuts. During test cuts, rods vibrated in the V-groove of the saw, which led to uneven cuts; I printed a clamping lever piece to secure them down, resulting in straighter cuts. To achieve precise overall length I added an adjustable screw stopper and length markings along the saw base surface.
  • Implementation. My final design made repeatable, accurate cuts. I wrote a maintenance manual and user guide. The saw was successfully used by the team working on the larger assembly through their building process.

Otoscope fiducial aligner

A device to visually align thin ceramic plates hosting cathodes within a larger assembly using their fiducial markers.

  • SolidWorks
  • 3D printing
  • Prototyping
  • Shop work
The problem

To achieve the best efficiency between electrons emitted from source cathodes on a ceramic plate and their target, the cathodes need to be as precisely aligned as possible with the layers of metal plates placed above them in their assembly part. I needed to find a way to achieve this without relying on the outer edges of the ceramic cathode plates, as they were not manufactured with precise enough tolerances. I utilized small fiducial markers printed onto the plates to visually align them within the assembly.

What I did

I started with hand sketching design concepts. I then researched different microscopes and small cameras needed to see the small fiducial marks. Finally, I went through multiple rounds of CAD and 3D printing different components of my device, testing and checking my design with model pieces of the cathode plates and larger assembly.

  • A fit for any gate. Each layer of the assembly had oval slots where the cathodes align and the electrons flow through. I created extruded oval shapes on the surface of my device to slide and lock into these slots and hold the larger assembly down. This allowed my device to work seamlessly with any of the larger gate assemblies, as they all had unique outer shapes, making other sorts of clamps difficult to make universal.
  • Seeing the fiducials. The geometry of the assembly is such that the fiducials can only be seen from the bottom of the assembly and through a tiny hole. After researching multiple camera options, I opted to use otoscopes. The small cameras, meant for looking in ears, worked well to see the underside of the assembly through the small hole. Additionally, by changing the lighting brightness on the cameras, I could eliminate glare from the metal assembly, making the fiducials easier to see.
  • Standalone by design. I chose otoscopes with individual displays and directly integrated them into my design. This let my device be completely standalone rather than needing to be plugged into a laptop or other device. Since my device was to be used by multiple people but always in the lab space, this was optimal.
  • Implementation. I wrote a user manual for my device and trained the team members on proper use. My device was placed in the team's lab and used to precisely align the cathode layered assemblies for the development of the computed tomography machine.

Fiber optic wire cutter

A machine and website system that cuts and marks fiber optic and copper wire to customizable lengths and marking patterns.

  • Arduino R4 WiFi
  • C++
  • HTML
  • SolidWorks
  • Soldering
  • Circuit Design
  • 3D printing
  • Servo & stepper motors
The problem

I needed to cut and mark fiber optic wires and copper electrical wires to specific lengths with certain markings. Multiple different configurations for each were necessary for the construction of a computed tomography machine. The wires needed were a mix of repeated lengths and fully unique lengths and marking patterns, with many versions of each needed. In order to achieve this, I created an automated cutting machine with a custom website interface for the user to customize each cut.

What I did

I wanted the cuts to be fully customizable. I originally thought of using buttons with preset cut lengths and marking patterns, then I remembered using an Arduino R4 WiFi in my circuits class and decided to utilize one for my design so I could host a web interface allowing for more control and customization. I started by hand sketching the physical components, CADing and printing them. I then moved to circuit design and built the electrical components. Finally, I coded the web interface in HTML and used C++ for the Arduino controls on the physical device.

  • Measuring the wires. I used a stepper motor to control a V-grooved driving wheel which pulls the cable off its spool. I used the geometry of the driving wheel and the number of steps the motor took to keep track of and determine the length of the wire. I added grip tape to the following wheel, which holds the wire down, to reduce slippage and keep the length accurate.
  • Marking. My initial sketches used a lever arm to control the marker. I later pivoted to using a servo motor and spring mechanism moving the marker horizontally, which allowed for quicker, more accurate marks and a smaller, more compact overall device design.
  • Web interface. I made a web interface which controls the device and allows users to customize their cut. The site has both preset buttons for set pattern lengths for markings and an option to manually enter placement of each mark. I added options for including slack length (before any marks), overall length, and quantity.
  • Implementation. I created detailed documentation, current status, next steps, and clear guide to the code. I finished the project up until testing the blade mechanism so I met with other team members who would continue the project to explain my steps and answer questions to allow for a seamless hand off.
